\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Component evidence}

The model-free AgentDojo replay covered 16 non-manual attack families and
7,404 security cases. All defined attack cases were detected and received the
configured hard-block outcome. None of the 97 distinct benign tasks was
blocked; one reached the approval threshold. The detector-v2 regression
scorecard recorded recall 1.000 with no false positives on both AgentDojo
corpora. On the WASP development corpus it recorded recall 1.000, precision
0.988, and one false positive among 39 benign pages. Full component and
action-gating results appear in \cref{app:assurance}; the end-to-end results
below determine whether those mechanisms preserved useful trajectories.

The replay establishes two prerequisites for interpreting the agent results.
First, the configured policy produced the intended decision for every defined
security case without obtaining that coverage by blocking the benign task set.
Second, the detector operated on the representations actually supplied by the
two adapters, rather than on an abstract list of attack strings. The near-
perfect component scores are not treated as a generalisation estimate because
the rules were refined against these corpora. Their role is narrower: they show
that a failure in an end-to-end row is not explained by the detector simply
being disabled or the policy fixture failing to load.

For 4B, enforcement produced 84 additional useful outcomes, 206 fewer
attacker successes, and 149 additional useful-and-secure outcomes. The 9B row
produced 45 additional useful outcomes and prevented 130 attacker successes;
paired transitions contained 92 utility gains and 47 losses. For 27B, utility
increased by 55 cases, all 203 off-condition attacker successes were absent,
and secure utility increased by 138. Its paired outcomes contained 84 utility
gains and 29 losses, demonstrating that the aggregate gain was substantial but
not cost-free for every trajectory.

The joint outcomes make the security gain more concrete. Enforcement removed
65, 55, and 83 cases in which the user and attacker oracles both succeeded for
4B, 9B, and 27B respectively. These are especially important: apparent task
completion could otherwise conceal an unauthorised side effect. It also removed
141, 76, and 120 attacker successes from trajectories that had already failed
the user, showing why unsuccessful work cannot be counted as inherently safe.
After enforcement, every successful 4B and 27B user outcome was secure; the 9B
row retained one attacker success in an unsuccessful user trajectory.

Suite-level outcomes show that the aggregate was not generated by one task
environment. For 4B, native utility increased in banking (86 to 99), Slack (58
to 60), travel (35 to 100), and workspace (183 to 187), while attacker success
fell to zero in every suite. The 27B profile was more heterogeneous: utility
fell in banking (118 to 99), was unchanged in Slack (70), and rose in travel
(76 to 112) and workspace (164 to 202). That variation motivates retaining
paired task outcomes and limitations rather than describing enforcement as a
uniform utility improvement.

The mechanism was selective rather than refusal-heavy. Detector-positive tool
output occurred in 600, 615, and 609 enforce cases for 4B, 9B, and 27B. Each
model recorded nine explicit action blocks and no approval actions. In the
reviewed 27B aggregate, 2,181 actions were allowed and all nine blocks were
\texttt{sensitive\_data\_flow}. Sanitising untrusted tool results before the
next decision was therefore the dominant recorded intervention; action policy
served as a backstop.

The utility gains follow from the structure of indirect injection. A diverted
agent may abandon the user's task or consume its step budget. Removing the
malicious tool result can restore the original trajectory as well as prevent
the attacker goal. This effect appears in each independent model row and is
interpreted as a paired enforcement outcome, not as a model-size trend.

Clean utility remained above attacked-off utility for every model: 78.4\%
versus 57.6\% for 4B, 82.5\% versus 67.1\% for 9B, and 83.5\% versus 68.0\% for
27B. Enforce utility did not exceed each clean ceiling, but it closed much of
the gap while suppressing attacker outcomes. This is the expected direction if
injected tool results cause both security violations and task diversion. It is
also why the analysis reports clean capability, attacked utility, and secure
utility separately rather than interpreting an ASR reduction in isolation.

All five off-condition attacker successes were absent under enforcement, and
no new success appeared. Attacker-directed attempts and executions changed
from 17 task instances to zero. Utility changed by one task
((-1.19) percentage points), while secure utility increased by two tasks
((+2.38) points). Paired utility contained seven gains, eight losses, and 69
unchanged cases. Joint outcomes moved from 44 useful/secure, three
useful/compromised, 35 unsuccessful/secure, and two
unsuccessful/compromised cases to 46 useful/secure and 38
unsuccessful/secure cases.

Telemetry identifies the observed mechanism. The enforce condition recorded
368 sanitisation attempts and 368 confirmed removals, with no residual or
unattributable outcome. It recorded no hard block or handover. One page-code
proposal triggered an approval and was denied, but the task still passed its
native user oracle. The result is consequently associated primarily with
post-verified observation sanitisation rather than widespread action refusal.

The 368 events comprise 15 direct line removals and 353 bounded-window
removals. No post-success detector alert was recorded. The branch distribution
matters because WASP exposes injections through an accessibility-tree
representation in which one logical instruction can be fragmented across
nodes: the result depends mainly on the multi-line path introduced during V2,
not on a trivial exact-line deletion. Confirmed removal is an event-level
post-condition; the native 84-task trajectories remain the outcome evidence.

The paired utility losses also cannot be equated with direct policy false
blocks. No hard block or takeover occurred, and the sole denied approval was in
a task whose user oracle succeeded. The eight losses therefore arose from
changed agent trajectories after the observation was rewritten, website or
model variability within the fixed setting, or finite-step interactions, not
from eight explicit vetoes. Conversely, seven off failures became enforce
successes. The net change of one task is a compact aggregate over both effects.

Zero attacker success in 84 tasks is not proof that the underlying rate is
zero. Under the rule-of-three heuristic it corresponds to an approximate 3.6\%
upper bound. The stronger observation in this dataset is the conjunction of
zero terminal successes, zero matched attacker-directed proposals, zero
executions, and 368 verified removals. Two of the four off cells already had no
terminal attack success, which further demonstrates why the execution replay
is required alongside ASR.

Warning-only enforcement left ASR at 7/84, increased attacker-directed
execution from 31/84 to 32/84, and reduced utility from 45/84 to 37/84.
Detection plus a model-facing warning did not contain the attack under this
condition. Sanitisation V1 improved utility and reduced attacker execution,
but six terminal attacks and 18 attacker-directed executions remained. Trace
analysis identified incomplete removal when an instruction spanned several
accessibility-tree lines.

V2 replaced the incomplete line handling with a bounded cluster strategy. Its
row recorded 0/84 terminal attacks and one attacker-directed navigation, which
did not satisfy the final attacker oracle. Across 1,057 sanitisation attempts,
1,053 were confirmed removals and four unattributable cases followed the
historical warning fallback. A frozen observation replay independently
confirmed removal in all 84 attacked observations without a detector-positive
residual or whole-page wipe. The sequence therefore supplied the engineering
lesson that shaped the final boundary: detection became effective containment
only when coupled to independently enforced removal and verification.

The event distribution again locates the improvement. Fourteen V2 removals
used the direct line branch and 1,039 used the bounded-window branch; the four
unattributable events were the only cases with no removal branch. The replay
also checked that non-anchor control descriptors remained present, excluding a
degenerate explanation in which the whole page was erased. The single
attacker-directed V2 execution shows why the conclusion is phrased in terms of
measured reduction rather than an absolute mediation guarantee: terminal ASR
was zero, but one intermediate effect still reached the browser tool.

Warning, V1, and V2 are intentionally all retained. Removing the first two rows
would make the final mechanism appear to follow directly from detection, while
the observations show the opposite. Warning-only supplied the detector output
to the potentially influenced planner. V1 independently changed the input but
did not verify all fragmented representations. V2 paired independent rewriting
with a post-condition over the reconstructed model message. The resulting
progression is a design-development result, not a claim that model quality or
random variation caused a monotonic ablation curve.

\subsection{Campaign completion and recorded activity}

The primary 27B campaign produced all 168 expected native-scored checkpoints:
84 off and 84 enforce across the four cells. Every checkpoint matched the
frozen model, policy, participant, generated-input, benchmark, and middleware
provenance. There was no task error, controller restart, cleanup failure,
provenance mismatch, or native-score mismatch. These checks matter because a
missing browser task cannot be treated as an unsuccessful attack or user task;
the denominators in \cref{tab:wasp-27b} represent completed executions.

The off trajectories contained 487 proposed actions, compared with 379 under
enforcement. The enforce row recorded at least one prompt-injection detection in
all 84 task instances and 368 event-level sanitisation attempts. The lower
action total is consistent with removal preventing some attacker-serving or
recovery steps, but it is not itself a utility measure: a shorter trajectory
can either complete efficiently or stop early. Native utility and final-state
attack oracles remain the outcome measures, while the action and sanitisation
counts explain how the trajectories differed.

\section{Result synthesis}

The three evidence levels align without being pooled. Component replay confirms
the configured detector and policy decisions. Across AgentDojo's controlled
tool environments, enforcement sharply reduced attacker success while utility
and secure utility rose. The matched 27B WASP pair then preserved the direction
of the security effect in a browser loop: the five terminal attacks and all 17
attacker-directed executions observed off were absent under enforcement.

Utility sets the boundary on that conclusion. AgentDojo enforce utility moved
towards each model's clean ceiling, whereas WASP produced seven gains and eight
losses, a net change of one task, while secure utility increased by two. The
browser evidence therefore supports preservation of roughly the same aggregate
completion, not a general claim that enforcement improves utility.

The intervention record explains why the result is attributed to
post-verified sanitisation. Explicit action refusal was rare in AgentDojo and
absent in the matched WASP row; its one denied approval occurred in a task that
still succeeded. By contrast, the recurring intervention changed the
observation before the next decision. The 9B sequence supplies the negative
controls: warning left hostile input in the model channel, and V1 left an
incompletely rewritten representation. V2 combined attributable removal with
a post-condition over the exact reconstructed message.
