\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Component evidence}
\label{sec:component-evidence}

Before interpreting agent behaviour, the evaluation first checks that the
configured guards behaved as intended. The model-free AgentDojo replay covered
7,404 security cases across 16 non-manual attack families. Every case was
detected and received the configured hard-block outcome. None of the 97
distinct benign tasks was hard-blocked, although one reached the approval
threshold. A separate detector-v2 scorecard then tested the exact text
representations produced by both adapters. Recall was 1.000 with no false
positives on the two AgentDojo corpora. On the WASP development corpus, recall
was 1.000 and precision was 0.988, with one false positive among 39 benign
pages. These WASP figures are detector-development evidence, not outcomes from
the final three-seed browser experiment. The evaluated policy allowed up to
four approval requests per task, preventing the agent from repeatedly
pressuring the operator. A hypothetical rule that requested approval for every
tainted action would have reached this limit in 35 of 50 affected tasks.
Sentinel's selective rule continued to gate cross-origin exfiltration but did
not reach the limit in any replayed task.

Together, the replay and scorecard establish two conditions for interpreting
the end-to-end results. First, the configured policy produced its intended
decision for every defined security case without hard-blocking the benign task
set. Second, the detector was tested on the text produced by the AgentDojo and
WASP adapters, not only on the original benchmark payloads. However, these
remain development-regression results because the rules were refined against
the same corpora; they do not estimate performance on unseen attacks. Their
narrower value is to confirm that later failures cannot be explained simply by
a detector that never fired or a policy fixture that failed to load. The
following sections can therefore examine whether the complete governed
configuration also preserved useful agent trajectories.

Finally, automated tests exercised approval revalidation, protected-value and
upload handoff, unresolved-observation withholding, timeouts, cancellation,
paired audit records, and redaction. Deterministic walkthroughs then traced
ordinary operation, approval, takeover, and incident states through the
production service and compiled Chrome extension. The checks confirmed
fail-closed authority transitions and redacted projection of server-owned
state; the manifest records zero model calls and confirms that no browser agent
or benchmark was started. These findings show that the implementation behaved
as designed; they do not measure operator comprehension, decision quality,
usability, or intervention burden. Reproduction commands and hashes appear in
\nolinkurl{thesis/FIGURE-PROVENANCE.md}.

The averages, however, conceal movement in both directions at the task level.
For 4B, the enforce condition added 84 useful outcomes, removed 206 attacker
successes, and added 149 useful-and-secure outcomes. With 9B, it added 45 useful
outcomes and prevented 130 attacker successes; across matched tasks, 92 failures
became successes while 47 successes were lost. Utility for 27B rose by 55 and
secure utility by 138, but that net change comprised 84 gains and 29 losses. An
aggregate improvement did not therefore mean that every trajectory benefited.

The headline rates become clearer when utility and attacker success are read
together. Each episode falls into one of four groups: useful and secure, useful
but compromised, unsuccessful and secure, or unsuccessful and compromised. In
off mode, utility exceeded secure utility by 65, 55, and 83 episodes for 4B,
9B, and 27B. These gaps are cases in which the model completed the user's task
but also achieved the attacker's goal; the enforce condition reduced all three
counts to zero. Nor did failure on the user's task imply safety. The attacker
still succeeded in 141, 76, and 120 such episodes in off mode, compared with
zero, one, and zero under enforcement. This four-way view therefore exposes
risk that either utility or attacker success alone would conceal.

Utility changes were also uneven across task suites. For 4B, utility increased
in banking (86 to 99), Slack (58 to 60), travel (35 to 100), and workspace (183
to 187), while attacker success fell to zero in every suite. By comparison,
the 27B profile fell in banking (118 to 99), remained unchanged in Slack (70),
and rose in travel (76 to 112) and workspace (164 to 202). The aggregate should
therefore not be read as a uniform utility improvement.

The telemetry also clarifies how Sentinel intervened: it rarely stopped actions
outright. Detector-positive tool output appeared in 600, 615, and 609 enforce
cases for 4B, 9B, and 27B, while each model recorded only nine explicit action
blocks and no approval actions. In the reviewed 27B aggregate, 2,181 actions
were allowed. The nine blocked actions were attempted password updates carrying
recognised credentials, recorded under the
\texttt{sensitive\_data\_flow} policy rule. Within the runtime telemetry,
sanitisation was therefore the most frequent external intervention, while
action policy served as a backstop.

This pattern offers a plausible explanation for the joint security and utility
gains. Indirect injection can pull the agent away from the user's task or
consume its step budget. Removing detected malicious content gives the planner
an opportunity to resume the original trajectory, while the fixed guidance
reinforces that tool results do not authorise a new goal. The model rows are
consistent with this combined account, but they do not identify the separate
contribution of either layer or establish a model-size trend.

Clean runs provide a final reference point. Clean utility remained above
attacked-off utility for every model: 78.4\% versus 57.6\% for 4B, 82.5\%
versus 67.1\% for 9B, and 83.5\% versus 68.0\% for 27B. Enforcement did not
return any model to its clean ceiling, but it recovered part of the gap while
suppressing attacker outcomes. These comparisons reinforce the need to report
clean capability, attacked utility, and secure utility together; an ASR
reduction alone would hide the distinction.

\section{WASP browser-native results}

\subsection{Replicated controlled comparison}

The browser-native study tests how Sentinel behaves when the agent must read
pages, navigate, act, and replan. For each of seeds 0, 1, and 2, the same 84
attacked tasks ran under off, policy-only, and full conditions. The campaign
therefore contained 252 episodes per condition and 756 overall, with every rate
in \cref{tab:wasp-27b} using 252 as its denominator.

The table connects final-state results with evidence from the action
trajectory. WASP's native oracle records completion of the attacker's goal,
while the independent audit checks for matching attacker-directed proposals
and executions. The two views agreed on terminal success: every native positive
also contained an executed attacker-directed action, so raw and audited ASR
were identical. The Attempted and Executed columns look earlier in the
trajectory by counting episodes with at least one matching proposal or action.
Potential false blocks remain an upper bound on disrupted legitimate work, not
confirmed causes of task failure.

The off-to-full comparison gives the clearest overall result. Terminal attacker
success fell from 17/252 to zero, and attacker-directed execution fell from

The security direction was consistent across all three seeds. Audited attacker

\subsection{What the policy-only/full contrast establishes}

The policy-only condition adds fixed security guidance, boundary neutralisation,
the detector warning, and action policy, but leaves browser sanitisation
disabled. Compared with off, it reduced audited attacker success from 17/252 to
1/252 and attacker-directed execution from 42/252 to 15/252. These reductions
reflect the controls working together and cannot be assigned to action policy
or guidance alone.

Full retains the fixed guidance and action controls while adding browser
sanitisation. Once removal is verified, it no longer sends the
detection-specific warning to the model. The shared fixed guidance means this
contrast tests an additional runtime observation treatment, not a change in
that instruction. Aggregate utility was unchanged at 149/252, while episodes
containing attacker-directed proposals and executions fell from 15/252 to zero.
Within every seed, both measures fell from five episodes to zero. Audited
attacker success also changed from 1/252 to zero, and secure utility rose from
148/252 to 149/252. The terminal ASR difference is only one episode because
policy-only completed just one attacker goal. The trajectory difference is
larger: policy-only produced attacker-directed proposals in 15 episodes,
whereas full produced none across 252 episodes.

The intervention logs help explain this result. Full performed 740
sanitisation attempts and verified removal in all 740 cases: 21 used direct-line
removal and 719 used bounded context windows. Every reconstructed observation
passed re-detection; none remained detector-positive or unattributable, and no
post-removal warning was sent to the model. Policy-only, with sanitisation
disabled, recorded no sanitisation events. It did raise two approval requests
in one episode. Since no human participated, the benchmark controller denied
both: one concerned an attacker-directed action and the other did not. The
latter is the single potential false block in \cref{tab:wasp-27b}. By
comparison, full produced no attacker-directed proposal and raised no approval,
block, handover, or security-pause event.

These were benchmark campaigns without human participants, so actual human
intervention is not applicable. Pre-execution mediation nevertheless averaged
below 0.6 milliseconds per governed decision in every condition, and no time
was spent waiting for human input. Although mean episode duration was lower
under enforcement, this does not show that Sentinel sped up the browser.
Episode duration includes model generation, browser actions, trajectory length,
and shutdown as well as enforcement; only the separately measured pre-execution
decision time estimates Sentinel's local mediation cost.

\subsection{Security by incompetence}

Terminal ASR alone gives an incomplete picture of baseline diversion. In the 252
off episodes, 42 executed an attacker-directed action, yet only 17 completed the
attacker goal. The other 25 are direct examples of security by incompetence: the
browser acted for the attacker, but the agent failed to carry the attack through.
One additional episode proposed a matching action that never executed. No such
action was recorded in the remaining 209 episodes. Those cases do not reveal why
the attack failed: the agent may not have encountered or recognised the
injection, may have broken down during navigation or replanning, or may simply
have stopped early.

By contrast, full enforcement recorded no attempted or executed
attacker-directed action. Even so, zero terminal successes in 252 episodes should
not be read as proof of a zero underlying rate. Under the rule-of-three
heuristic, this result gives an approximate 95\% upper bound of 1.19\%, assuming
independent binomial trials. The benchmark repeats a fixed set of tasks across
seeds, however, so this figure is a descriptive bound for the experiment, not an
estimate of deployment risk. The conclusion therefore rests not on the zero
alone, but on its agreement with the independent action audit, the consistent
direction across seeds, and 740 verified removals.

\subsection{Campaign integrity}

All 756 final episodes received native scores. The validator checked all 36
cells against their seed, condition, input manifest, model, policy, and
middleware record; every cell passed, with no restart, incomplete checkpoint,
provenance mismatch, task error, or structured-output failure. A pristine
website reset preceded each cell. Finally, the fixed sequence was repeatable,
yet changes in service load, model-serving state, or other runtime conditions
could still coincide with a later condition.

\section{Result synthesis}

Although the three evaluation levels answer different questions and are not
pooled, they point in the same direction. Component replay tests the guards
directly; AgentDojo measures native outcomes in controlled tool environments,
while WASP tests transfer into a browser-native loop. Security must still be
read with utility and paired trajectories. Chapter~7 interprets this evidence,
including the sanitisation result and its limits.
