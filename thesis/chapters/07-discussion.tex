\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Answer to the research question}

In the evaluated models, tasks, and textual channels, Sentinel's complete
governed configuration reduced prompt-injection-driven violations while
retaining useful work. Its independence is architectural: model-facing guidance
may shape a proposal, but observation and execution decisions remain under the
authority of a separate runtime. The scope of that answer matters: Sentinel does
not solve prompt injection, and the case rests on complementary AgentDojo and
WASP evaluations, not on any single zero-success run.

Across three matched AgentDojo configurations, the complete configuration
reduced attacker success while increasing utility and secure utility. In
browser-native WASP, full enforcement reduced action-audited terminal success
from 17/252 to zero and attacker-directed execution from 42/252 to zero.
Legitimate completion fell from 154/252 to 149/252, while secure utility rose
from 138/252 to 149/252 (\cref{tab:agentdojo-e2e,tab:wasp-27b}). Thus,
AgentDojo utility improved while WASP incurred a small aggregate cost, but
neither showed a collapse in useful completion.

Their rates are not directly comparable because their interfaces, attacks, and
oracles differ. The shared architectural lesson is that the model can plan
useful actions while a separate runtime governs which untrusted observations
reach it and which proposed privileged actions are executed.

\section{Interpreting security and utility}

The main empirical result is that lower attacker success did not come from
refusing every attacked task. Indirect prompt injection competes with the
user's goal for context, steps, and later decisions. Removing detected hostile
content before the agent plans again may therefore help it return to useful
work while also suppressing the attacker's goal. The AgentDojo utility gains
fit this explanation. WASP, however, recorded a small aggregate utility loss
that varied across seeds, so the benefit was neither automatic nor uniform.
The experiments did not isolate intervention timing, which means this account
remains an interpretation of the combined results, not a demonstrated causal
mechanism.

The utility measures answer different questions. Clean utility estimates what a
model can achieve when no attack is present, but it does not show how the same
attacked task would fare without enforcement. The relevant comparison comes
from matched off/enforce or off/full runs. Secure utility is stricter: it counts
an episode only when the user's objective succeeds and the attacker's does not.
A defence can therefore reduce ordinary utility yet improve secure utility by
removing compromised completions. At the same time, an aggregate utility gain
can hide regressions on individual tasks. Security and usefulness need to be
read together, with paired gains and losses providing the link between them.

Terminal attack success tells only part of the story. In the WASP baseline, 42
episodes executed an attacker-directed action, yet only 17 reached the final
attacker objective. In the other 25, the browser had already acted for the
attacker, but the agent failed to complete the goal. WASP describes this as
``security by incompetence''~\cite{evtimov2025wasp}. Action-level telemetry
makes this hidden failure visible and separates a model proposal from an effect
that crossed the privileged boundary. The replay still has limits: it matches
the attacker goals instantiated by the benchmark and cannot identify
reasoning-only diversion or every semantically equivalent action.

The same evidence also cautions against simple cross-model comparisons: a more
capable agent may complete more legitimate and malicious goals, while a weaker
one can appear secure simply because it fails at both. The evaluated
configurations also differ in planning and recovery behaviour, not just in
parameter count.
Model size and a low off-condition attack rate therefore cannot be treated as
independent measures of security. A defensible comparison stays within each
matched model and considers useful completion, attacker outcomes, and executed
effects together.

\section{What the two enforcement boundaries contribute}

Observation and action governance protect different points in the agent loop.
The observation boundary governs what reaches the planner; the action boundary
governs which proposals reach the privileged executor. Both matter, as an
injected instruction can distort a trajectory before it produces an obviously
prohibited action, while an apparently safe observation says nothing about
whether the eventual action will be permissible.

The WASP policy-only/full comparison gives the clearest evidence for the
observation boundary. Both conditions shared the fixed security guidance,
boundary neutralisation, and action policy. Policy-only retained detected
content and issued a warning, whereas full added verified browser sanitisation
and removed that warning after confirmed removal. The comparison therefore
isolates the configured sanitisation increment within a shared guidance
scaffold; it is not a pure action-policy ablation. Aggregate utility remained
149/252 in both conditions, but attacker-directed proposals and executions fell
from 15/252 to zero, while terminal attacker success changed from 1/252 to zero
(\cref{tab:wasp-27b,tab:wasp-seeds}). The trajectory evidence matters because a
terminal score alone would hide most of the difference.

Sanitisation, however, does not remove the need for action governance: a detector
can miss an unfamiliar instruction, a proposal may depend on earlier state, and
an unrelated model error can still reach a privileged tool.
Pre-execution policy can enforce stable constraints in those cases without
reconstructing the model's full intention; the sensitive-data-flow blocks in
AgentDojo illustrate that backstop. The evaluation did not include a comparator
that passed hostile content to the model unchanged and attempted to contain the
attack solely through pre-execution action checks. The results therefore show
that the two boundaries provided different forms of control in the tested
configuration; they do not establish that observation sanitisation is always
more effective than action-only enforcement.

Together, the two boundaries show what model independence means in practice.
Sentinel does not need access to a model's hidden reasoning or a special
security interface. It gives the planner a governed observation, receives an
ordinary BrowserUse tool proposal, and then checks facts available to the
runtime before execution, including protected-value classes, local-file
properties, target origins, and live browser state. As a result, the same
enforcement boundary can govern proposals from different compatible models.
This compatibility does not make the models interchangeable: each may
interpret the governed observation and complete the remaining task with a
different level of success.

\section{Implications for agent architecture}

Sentinel's place among the systems in \cref{tab:system-comparison} reflects a
deliberate architectural choice. CaMeL and Fides offer stronger formal
guarantees within their supported abstractions by restructuring planning
around trusted programs, capabilities, or information-flow labels. Sentinel
instead preserves BrowserUse's ordinary proposal interface and governs the
observations and actions that cross it. This choice makes enforcement
compatible with a free-form browser loop, although its assurance remains
empirical and policy-bounded. The experiments show how the complete governed
configuration behaved under the tested conditions; they do not provide a
formal guarantee for every browser trajectory.

The limits of this choice are clearest when an action is permitted by encoded
policy but its purpose remains ambiguous. A legitimate task and an injected
goal can call for the same form, button, or origin. Consequently, provenance
alone cannot establish whether the effect serves the operator. Sentinel can
enforce explicit constraints on domains, protected-data handling, unsafe
navigation schemes, and configured approval thresholds. It cannot prove that
every allowed click advances an open-ended goal, and an unfamiliar instruction
may still evade detection. Task Shield, MELON, and DRIFT suggest a complementary
direction by testing actions against task-derived or counterfactual
representations~\cite{jia2025taskshield,zhu2025melon,li2025drift}. An isolated
semantic monitor could assess a proposal against a trusted task representation,
while Sentinel retains final authority over deterministic policy and live
browser state. Such a hybrid would be useful only if it improved coverage
without excessive false intervention or loss of useful autonomy.

Selective human authority addresses a related class of uncertainty. Some
operations are permissible but consequential, while others require the
operator to act directly. Sentinel therefore distinguishes narrow approval, a
direct human step, hard refusal, and quarantine of an unresolved observation.
Each state grants different authority, so collapsing them into one confirmation
prompt would obscure whether the agent, the operator, or neither may proceed.
Tests and walkthroughs establish the mechanics of these paths, including
bounded approval, live revalidation, audit, and fail-closed resolution
(\cref{sec:human-authority}). They do not show whether people will interpret
the distinctions correctly or avoid warning fatigue. The architecture supplies
the technical basis for a future human study, although the effectiveness of
these distinctions in practice remains an open empirical question.

\section{Limitations and validity}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

Sentinel's main security limitation is adaptation. Several attack families
shaped detection and representation handling during development. For example,
one WASP failure exposed an unsafe link target omitted from BrowserUse's
accessibility text. Sentinel's external architecture provides a separate route
for responding to newly identified attack patterns: the relevant controls can
be revised and tested without changing the model itself. This does not make
adaptation automatic: each change requires renewed security and utility
evaluation, and the update path does not imply protection against attacks that
remain unrecognised. Likewise, a clean post-sanitisation check shows only that
the detector no longer fires, not that hostile intent is absent. Adaptive-attack
research, including MUZZLE~\cite{zhan2025adaptive,syros2026muzzle}, suggests a
stronger test: freeze the system, withhold attack families during development,
then use agent behaviour to select injection surfaces and execution feedback to
refine attacks.

The measurements add two limits. First, WASP's action audit recognises the
benchmark's attacker goals but may miss equivalent effects and reasoning-only
diversion. Second, the model's WASP observation path excluded screenshots. The
findings therefore apply to the evaluated textual channel, not pixel-only
instructions or visually grounded tasks. Extending Sentinel to images would
require a separate design for provenance, isolation, and action authority,
since visual input adds both capability and attack surface
~\cite{bailey2024imagehijacks,koh2024visualwebarena}.

The experimental design also limits generalisability. AgentDojo uses three
matched model configurations, whereas the final WASP campaign tests one 27B
model across three seeds and a fixed 84-case matrix. The evidence is therefore
strongest within those configurations and does not show how Sentinel would
perform across a wider range of models or deployment settings. Website state
was reset before each cell, but fixed condition order means changes in service
load or model-serving state may have affected later conditions. AgentDojo's
end-to-end comparison uses only the baseline and complete governed
configuration, so it cannot isolate each control's contribution.

The prototype cannot observe every risk. Its protected-data checks recognise
familiar patterns but may miss sensitive information whose meaning is clear
only from context. Sentinel can inspect an action directed to the current
website before execution, but cannot observe consequences produced later by the
server. The local API accepts only connections from the same machine, yet
another process under the same user account could still reach it. Tests cover
incident handling, secret takeover, live revalidation, extraction and history
handling, and special-URL controls. The campaigns did not exercise every
mechanism; these tests therefore demonstrate implementation behaviour, not new
benchmark results.

\subsection{Ethics and responsible research}
\label{subsec:ethics}

The thesis deliberately makes no claims about human factors. No human
participated in the reported studies, and no participant data were collected.
Scripted benchmark modes provided reproducible control behaviour, so approval
counts, pause duration, and Activity annotations are technical telemetry.
Interface walkthroughs verify server-owned request semantics and rendered
states, but do not measure comprehension, trust, workload, habituation, or
recovery quality.

Research and reporting choices also reduce unnecessary exposure. Browser
evidence came from controlled benchmarks and local synthetic fixtures. The
submitted figures contain no live credentials, private accounts, private URLs,
or raw attack payloads; nor does the dissertation reproduce the benchmark's
operational injection text. These safeguards limit harmful disclosure without
removing the dual-use risk: the analysis could inform attacks as well as
defences. Any future operator study would require prior ethics approval,
consent, data minimisation, and secure record handling.

\section{Research priorities and broader implications}

The limitations suggest a clear order for future work. First, evaluation should
freeze the complete system, test attack families excluded from development, use
more seeds, vary condition order, and allow an adaptive attacker to respond to
agent behaviour. An action-only comparator would test whether removing hostile
content before planning preserves more useful progress than blocking harmful
actions at execution.

Next, extending the boundary beyond text requires trusted provenance, isolation
of uncertain content, and bounded action authority. An operator-visible
contract could state expected sites, data classes, effects, human steps, and
stopping conditions before untrusted content appears; an isolated semantic
monitor could compare proposals against it. Both require separate evidence
because the present results cover only textual observations.

Human and deployment questions remain separate priorities. A participant study
could test whether operators distinguish approval, direct action, and incident
review, then resume without excessive burden or warning fatigue; the control
plane provides only the technical basis. Deployment requires authenticated
access, stronger process isolation, protected policy and trace keys, and
transactional browser support.
