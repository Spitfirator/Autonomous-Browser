\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Answer to the research question}

The evidence gives a qualified but positive answer to the research question.
In the evaluated settings, independent runtime enforcement reduced
prompt-injection-driven violations and still allowed the agent to complete
useful work. The scope of that answer matters. It applies to the models, tasks,
and textual observation channels tested here; it does not mean that Sentinel
solves prompt injection. The case rests on two complementary evaluations, not
on any single run in which attacker success reached zero.

AgentDojo first shows this under controlled conditions. Across all three
matched model configurations, enforcement reduced attacker success and
increased utility and secure utility. WASP then asks how the same approach fares
in a browser-native loop, where the agent must read pages, navigate, and act.
Across three seeds, full enforcement reduced action-audited terminal success
from 17/252 episodes to zero and attacker-directed execution from 42/252 to
zero. Legitimate completion fell slightly, from 154/252 to 149/252, while secure
utility increased from 138/252 to 149/252
(\cref{tab:agentdojo-e2e,tab:wasp-27b}). The utility effects differ: AgentDojo
improved task completion, whereas WASP incurred a small aggregate cost. Even
so, both evaluations show the same broad outcome: violations fell sharply
without a collapse in useful task completion.

The two benchmarks should not be compared rate for rate, since their
interfaces, attack families, and success oracles differ. What matters is that
both point to the same architectural lesson. The planner remained free to
choose useful actions, but it was not left to decide which observations shaped
the next plan or which effects reached the executor. Sentinel separates
capability from authority. The model plans; an independent runtime governs the
points at which untrusted information enters and privileged effects leave the
agent loop.

\section{Interpreting security and utility}

The main empirical result is that lower attacker success did not come from
refusing every attacked task. Indirect prompt injection competes with the
user's goal for context, steps, and later decisions. Removing detected hostile
content before the agent plans again may therefore help it return to useful
work while also suppressing the attacker's goal. The AgentDojo utility gains
fit this explanation. WASP, however, recorded a small aggregate utility loss
that varied across seeds, so the benefit was neither automatic nor uniform.
The experiments did not isolate intervention timing, which means this account
remains an interpretation of the combined results, not a demonstrated causal
mechanism.

The utility measures answer different questions. Clean utility estimates what a
model can achieve when no attack is present, but it does not show how the same
attacked task would fare without enforcement. The relevant comparison comes
from matched off/enforce or off/full runs. Secure utility is stricter: it counts
an episode only when the user's objective succeeds and the attacker's does not.
A defence can therefore reduce ordinary utility yet improve secure utility by
removing compromised completions. At the same time, an aggregate utility gain
can hide regressions on individual tasks. Security and usefulness need to be
read together, with paired gains and losses providing the link between them.

Terminal attack success tells only part of the story. In the WASP baseline, 42
episodes executed an attacker-directed action, yet only 17 reached the final
attacker objective. In the other 25, the browser had already acted for the
attacker, but the agent failed to complete the goal. WASP describes this as
``security by incompetence''~\cite{evtimov2025wasp}. Action-level telemetry
makes this hidden failure visible and separates a model proposal from an effect
that crossed the privileged boundary. The replay still has limits: it matches
the attacker goals instantiated by the benchmark and cannot identify
reasoning-only diversion or every semantically equivalent action.

The same evidence also cautions against simple cross-model comparisons. A more
capable agent may complete more legitimate and malicious goals; a weaker one
can appear secure simply because it fails at both. The evaluated configurations
also differ in planning and recovery behaviour, not just in parameter count.
Model size and a low off-condition attack rate therefore cannot be treated as
independent measures of security. A defensible comparison stays within each
matched model and considers useful completion, attacker outcomes, and executed
effects together.

\section{What the two enforcement boundaries contribute}

Observation and action governance protect different points in the agent loop.
The observation boundary governs what reaches the planner; the action boundary
governs which proposals reach the privileged executor. Both matter. An injected
instruction can distort a trajectory before it produces an obviously
prohibited action, while an apparently safe observation says nothing about
whether the eventual action will be permissible.

The WASP policy-only/full comparison gives the clearest evidence for the
observation boundary. Policy-only retained boundary neutralisation, the
detector warning, and action policy, whereas full added verified browser
sanitisation and removed the warning after confirmed removal. The comparison
therefore isolates the configured sanitisation increment within that fixed
scaffold; it is not a pure action-policy ablation. Aggregate utility remained
149/252 in both conditions, but attacker-directed proposals and executions fell
from 15/252 to zero, while terminal attacker success changed from 1/252 to zero
(\cref{tab:wasp-27b,tab:wasp-seeds}). The trajectory evidence matters here: a
terminal score alone would hide most of the difference. An earlier 9B
development comparison points in the same direction: its combined
policy-and-warning condition left attack success unchanged and did not reduce
attacker-directed execution while hostile content remained in context.

Sanitisation, however, does not remove the need for action governance. A
detector can miss an unfamiliar instruction, a proposal may depend on earlier
state, and an unrelated model error can still reach a privileged tool.
Pre-execution policy can enforce stable constraints in those cases without
reconstructing the model's full intention; the sensitive-data-flow blocks in
AgentDojo illustrate that backstop. The evaluation did not include a comparator
that passed hostile content to the model unchanged and attempted to contain the
attack solely through pre-execution action checks. The results therefore show
that the two boundaries provided different forms of control in the tested
configuration; they do not establish that observation sanitisation is always
more effective than action-only enforcement.

Together, the boundaries also explain the practical sense in which Sentinel is
model-independent. The planner receives a governed observation and returns an
ordinary BrowserUse tool proposal; it need not expose hidden reasoning or
implement a security-specific interface. The runtime can then evaluate
deterministic context such as protected-value classes, local file properties,
target origins, and live browser state. Model independence here means
compatibility at the enforcement interface. It does not mean equal performance
across models, since the model still determines how well the remaining task is
planned.

\section{Implications for agent architecture}

Sentinel's place among the systems in \cref{tab:system-comparison} is defined
by a deliberate trade-off. CaMeL and Fides offer stronger formal guarantees
within their supported abstractions by restructuring planning around trusted
programs, capabilities, or information-flow labels. Sentinel instead preserves
BrowserUse's ordinary proposal interface and governs the observations and
actions that cross it. This choice makes enforcement compatible with a
free-form browser loop, although its assurance remains empirical and
policy-bounded. The experiments show how the complete governed configuration
behaved under the tested conditions; they do not provide a formal guarantee for
every browser trajectory.

The trade-off is clearest when an action is permitted by encoded policy but its
purpose remains ambiguous. A legitimate task and an injected goal can call for
the same form, button, or origin. Consequently, provenance alone cannot
establish whether the effect serves the operator. Sentinel can enforce explicit
constraints on domains, protected-data handling, unsafe navigation schemes,
and configured approval thresholds. It cannot prove that every allowed click
advances an open-ended goal, and an unfamiliar instruction may still evade
detection. Task Shield, MELON, and DRIFT suggest a complementary direction by
testing actions against task-derived or counterfactual representations
~\cite{jia2025taskshield,zhu2025melon,li2025drift}. An isolated semantic monitor
could assess a proposal against a trusted task representation, while Sentinel
retains final authority over deterministic policy and live browser state. Such
a hybrid would be useful only if it improved coverage without excessive false
intervention or loss of useful autonomy.

Selective human authority addresses a related class of uncertainty. Some
operations are permissible but consequential, while others require the
operator to act directly. Sentinel therefore distinguishes narrow approval, a
direct human step, hard refusal, and quarantine of an unresolved observation.
Each state grants different authority, so collapsing them into one confirmation
prompt would obscure whether the agent, the operator, or neither may proceed.
Tests and walkthroughs establish the mechanics of these paths, including
bounded approval, live revalidation, audit, and fail-closed resolution
(\cref{sec:human-authority}). They do not show whether people will interpret
the distinctions correctly or avoid warning fatigue. The architecture supplies
the technical basis for a future human study; its human effectiveness remains
an open empirical question.

\section{Limitations and validity}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

The principal security limitation concerns adaptation. Several attack families
informed detector and representation changes during development, including a
WASP failure in which an unsafe link target was absent from BrowserUse's
accessibility text. The component scorecards therefore demonstrate regression
coverage of known families, not generalisation to unseen attacks. The final
three-seed experiment has a stronger boundary because its middleware and
condition-specific policies were frozen before execution. Even there, however,
a clean post-sanitisation check establishes only that the configured detector no
longer fires; it does not prove that the remaining observation is free of
hostile intent. A held-out adaptive study against the frozen whole system is
needed to test that stronger claim. Browser-specific evaluation could follow
MUZZLE by selecting injection surfaces from agent behaviour and refining attacks
through execution feedback~\cite{zhan2025adaptive,syros2026muzzle}.

The observation and outcome measures impose further construct limits. WASP's
action audit recognises instantiated attacker goals but not every equivalent
effect or reasoning-only diversion. Moreover, screenshots were excluded from
the model's WASP observation path. The results consequently apply to the
evaluated textual channel and do not extend to pixel-only instructions or tasks
that require visual grounding. A visual path would need its own provenance,
isolation, and action-authority design because images expand both capability and
attack surface~\cite{bailey2024imagehijacks,koh2024visualwebarena}.

Experimental validity is bounded by the selected models, benchmarks, and
execution design. AgentDojo contributes three matched model configurations,
whereas the final WASP comparison uses one 27B model, three sampling seeds, and
a fixed 84-case matrix. Website state was reset before every cell, but condition
order was not randomised; changes in service load or model-serving state could
therefore coincide with a later condition. The historical 9B WASP rows remain
development evidence, and some AgentDojo artefacts predate the final provenance
schema. In addition, AgentDojo enforcement combines external guards with fixed
system-level security guidance, so its rates describe the governed
configuration as a whole rather than the guards in isolation. These constraints
preclude population-level deployment claims, causal model-scaling claims, and
fine-grained attribution of every observed effect.

Finally, the policy and prototype boundaries are finite. Pattern-based
protected-data classification can miss a semantically sensitive value, while a
permitted same-origin action may trigger server-side consequences unavailable
to local inspection. Loopback origin checks are appropriate for the local
prototype but do not isolate it from a compromised same-user process. Incidents,
secret takeover, live revalidation, extraction and history handling, and
special-URL controls have current test coverage, yet the frozen benchmark
trajectories did not measure their effects. They remain implementation assurance
rather than retrospective experimental evidence.

\subsection{Ethics and responsible research}
\label{subsec:ethics}

No human participated in the reported studies, and no participant data were
collected. Scripted benchmark modes supplied reproducible control behaviour, so
approval counts, pause duration, and Activity annotations are technical
telemetry. Likewise, interface walkthroughs establish server-owned request
semantics and rendered states, not comprehension, trust, workload, habituation,
or recovery quality.

The browser evidence came from controlled benchmark environments and local
synthetic interface fixtures. The submitted figures contain no live credential,
private account, private URL, or raw attack payload, and the dissertation does
not reproduce the benchmark's operational injection text. These choices reduce
unnecessary disclosure, although the work remains dual use because the analysis
can inform attacks as well as defences. Any future study of operator decisions
would therefore require ethics approval before recruitment or data collection,
together with protocols for consent, data minimisation, and secure handling of
records.

\section{Research priorities and broader implications}

The limitations suggest an order for future work rather than an undifferentiated
list of extensions. The first priority is a frozen, held-out adaptive evaluation
of the combined boundaries using unseen prompt families, a larger seed set, and
randomised or counterbalanced condition order. Adding a genuine action-only
comparator would also test whether early observation intervention preserves
useful trajectories more effectively than recognising harmful effects after the
model has planned them. This experiment would address the most important open
security and causal questions before expanding the prototype's capabilities.

The second priority is to broaden what can be governed. A visual guard would
need bounded, provenance-aware representations and stricter authority for
uncertain pixel-derived actions. In parallel, an operator-visible task contract
could state expected origins, data classes, permitted effects, human steps, and
stop conditions before exposure to untrusted content. Coupling that contract to
an isolated semantic monitor would test whether task-level reasoning can close
gaps left by deterministic policy without creating prohibitive false
interventions. Each mechanism would require its own evaluation rather than
borrowing confidence from the present textual results.

Human and deployment questions should follow the same evidential discipline. A
participant study could test whether operators understand interventions,
distinguish among approval, direct action, and incident review, and resume work
without excessive burden or warning fatigue. The implemented control plane is a
technical basis for that study, not evidence of those outcomes. Deployment
beyond the local prototype would separately require authenticated control,
stronger process separation, protected policy and trace keys, and support for
transactional browser environments. Better human interaction, broader attack
coverage, and stronger isolation are related goals, but evidence for one cannot
substitute for the others.

The broader implication is therefore architectural rather than universal.
Under the tested conditions, useful autonomy and independent authority were
compatible: Sentinel constrained what reached and left the planner without
requiring a perfect classifier or preventing the agent from completing useful
work. The results do not remove the need for stronger semantic representations,
adaptive testing, or human evaluation. They do show why authority need not be
entrusted to the same probabilistic component that plans the task.
