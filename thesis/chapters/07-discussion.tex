\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Answer to the research question}

Taken together, the findings support a qualified but positive answer to the
research question. Under the tested conditions, independent runtime enforcement
reduced prompt-injection-driven violations without removing the agent's ability
to complete useful work. This claim is deliberately narrower than saying that
Sentinel solves prompt injection. It is limited to the tested models, tasks,
and textual observation channels. The conclusion draws on converging evidence
from two complementary evaluations, not on any single zero-attack result.

AgentDojo provides the broader controlled comparison: across all three matched
model configurations, enforcement reduced attacker success and increased both
utility and secure utility. WASP then tests whether this broader
security--utility result carries over to a browser-native loop. Across three
seeds, full enforcement reduced
action-audited terminal success from 17/252 episodes to zero and
attacker-directed execution from 42/252 to zero. Although legitimate completion
fell slightly, from 154/252 to 149/252, secure utility rose from 138/252 to
149/252 (\cref{tab:agentdojo-e2e,tab:wasp-27b}). The benchmarks therefore do not
show identical utility effects. They do, however, support the same broader
conclusion: evaluated violations fell sharply while useful task completion
remained close to the baseline in aggregate.

The benchmarks use different interfaces, attack families, and success oracles,
so their absolute rates should not be pooled. Their agreement is most
informative at the architectural level. In both settings, the planner remained
free to choose useful actions, but it was not the sole authority over which
observations could shape its next plan or which effects could reach the
executor. The central implication is therefore a separation of model capability
from execution authority: the model plans, while an independent runtime governs
the points at which untrusted information enters and privileged effects leave
the agent loop.

\section{Interpreting security and utility}

The most important empirical pattern is not simply that attacker success fell,
but that containment did not require blanket refusal. Indirect prompt injection
competes with the user's task for context, steps, and subsequent decisions.
Removing detected hostile influence before the next plan therefore offers a
plausible explanation for why enforcement can recover useful work as well as
suppress an attacker goal. The AgentDojo utility gains are consistent with this
account. However, WASP's small aggregate utility loss and its variation between
seeds show that the benefit is neither automatic nor uniform. Because the
experiments did not isolate intervention timing, this remains an interpretation
of the joint results rather than an independently demonstrated causal effect.

This distinction also determines the appropriate counterfactual. Clean utility
estimates what a model can achieve without an attack; it does not reveal what
the same attacked trajectory would have achieved without enforcement. That
question is addressed by the matched off/enforce or off/full comparison. Secure
utility adds a further requirement by counting only episodes in which the
user's objective succeeds and the attacker's does not. Consequently, a defence
can lower ordinary utility yet still raise secure utility if it removes
compromised completions, just as an aggregate utility gain can conceal
task-level regressions. Security and usefulness should therefore be interpreted
together, supported by paired gains and losses rather than opposing headline
rates.

Terminal attack success is necessary but, for the same reason, insufficient.
In the WASP baseline, 42 episodes executed an attacker-directed action, whereas
only 17 completed the final attacker objective. The remaining 25 are direct
examples of the benchmark's ``security by incompetence'': the browser had
already acted for the attacker, but the agent failed to complete the goal
~\cite{evtimov2025wasp}. Action-level telemetry exposes this otherwise hidden
failure and distinguishes a model proposal from an effect that crossed the
privileged boundary. Nevertheless, the replay is conservative. It matches the
instantiated attacker goals and cannot identify reasoning-only diversion or
every semantically equivalent action.

This joint view also limits cross-model interpretation. A more capable agent
may complete more legitimate and malicious goals, while a weaker agent can
appear secure because it fails at both. Moreover, the evaluated configurations
differ in planning and recovery behaviour as well as parameter count. Neither
model size nor a low off-condition attack rate is therefore an independent
security axis. The defensible comparison remains within each matched model and
reports useful completion, attacker outcomes, and executed effects together.

\section{What the two enforcement boundaries contribute}

Observation and action governance address related but distinct failure modes.
The observation boundary changes the information on which the next plan is
conditioned; the action boundary decides whether a concrete proposal may cross
into the privileged executor. This distinction matters because an injected
instruction can damage the trajectory before it produces an obviously
prohibited action, whereas a safe-looking observation cannot guarantee that the
model's eventual proposal is permissible.

The policy-only/full contrast provides the clearest evidence for this division.
Policy-only is not a pure action-policy baseline: it retains boundary
neutralisation, a detector warning, and action policy, while full adds the
verified browser rewrite and suppresses the warning after confirmed removal.
Within that fixed scaffold, the comparison isolates the configured sanitisation
increment. Aggregate utility remained 149/252 in both conditions, but episodes
with attacker-directed proposals and executions fell from 15/252 under
policy-only to zero under full; terminal attacker success changed from 1/252 to
zero (\cref{tab:wasp-27b,tab:wasp-seeds}). The larger trajectory difference
would therefore be almost invisible if evaluation stopped at the terminal
oracle.

Historical development evidence reinforces this interpretation without turning
it into a causal ablation. In the 9B sequence, retaining hostile content while
warning the model did not improve terminal or intermediate attack outcomes over
off. Because the middleware also changed across that sequence, the warning's
independent effect cannot be estimated. Its value is instead negative and
design-oriented: it shows why a warning around retained hostile content should
not be treated as equivalent to attributable removal followed by re-detection.

At the same time, successful sanitisation does not make action governance
redundant. A detector may miss an unfamiliar instruction, a proposal may depend
on earlier state, and an unrelated model error can reach the same privileged
tool. Immediate pre-execution policy can still enforce stable constraints
without reconstructing the model's full intention. The few
sensitive-data-flow blocks recorded in the AgentDojo enforce runs illustrate
this backstop. Conversely, action-only enforcement would leave hostile content
inside the planning context and require policy to recognise its effects one by
one. That action-only system was not evaluated here, so the results do not show
that sanitisation generally outperforms later vetoes. They show more narrowly
that, in the tested configuration, the two boundaries contributed different
forms of control.

Their composition also clarifies Sentinel's model independence. The planner
need not expose hidden reasoning or implement a security-specific interface; it
receives a governed observation and returns the ordinary BrowserUse tool
proposal. Sentinel can then evaluate deterministic context that would be
unsuitable to delegate through the prompt, including protected-value classes,
local file properties, target origins, and live browser state. Model
independence therefore means compatibility at the enforcement interface, not
equal performance across models: the model still determines how well the
remaining task is planned.

\section{Implications for agent architecture}

Sentinel occupies a deliberate middle ground among the systems compared in
\cref{tab:system-comparison}. It does not inherit CaMeL's program-based or
Fides's information-flow guarantees: it preserves a free-form browser loop
rather than introducing either trusted control and data flow or formal label
propagation. Its contribution is correspondingly empirical: independently
governed observation rewriting, immediate pre-tool enforcement, selective
human authority, and paired audit operated together while retaining useful task
performance. Structured planners can offer stronger guarantees where their
trusted abstraction applies; an executor-side wrapper offers a practical
control point when an existing agent exposes no security-specific reasoning
interface.

That practical position also exposes a semantic limit. In WASP, legitimate and
attacker-directed trajectories often converge on the same-origin forms and
controls. Treating every effect following a detector finding as dangerous would
turn untrusted provenance into broad denial of service. Sentinel instead
reserves deterministic rules for independently sensitive sinks, including
prohibited cross-origin flow, protected values, unsafe schemes, local files,
code, and configured consequential targets. These rules can establish
compliance with encoded constraints, but they cannot prove that every allowed
effect advances an open-ended user goal. A natural-looking instruction may
evade detection, and a locally permissible click may have consequences that are
not visible in the DOM.

This gap suggests a complementary rather than replacement architecture. Task
Shield, MELON, and DRIFT use task-derived or counterfactual abstractions to
reason about semantic alignment
~\cite{jia2025taskshield,zhu2025melon,li2025drift}. An independently isolated
component could use a trusted task representation to assess ambiguous effects,
while Sentinel continues to enforce non-negotiable executor policy and binds
authority to live browser state. Such a hybrid could reduce reliance on surface
signatures without returning the final decision to the acting model. Its value
would depend, however, on whether the semantic monitor adds meaningful coverage
without enough false intervention to erase the utility retained here.

Selective human authority addresses a related boundary: some operations are
permissible yet consequential, while others require the operator rather than
the agent to act. Narrow approval, direct takeover, hard refusal, and unresolved
observation quarantine are therefore distinct authority states, not variations
of one confirmation dialogue. Server-owned scope, expiry, live revalidation,
and paired audit preserve that distinction (\cref{sec:human-authority}). Tests
and walkthroughs establish that these mechanisms fail closed and record their
outcomes. They do not establish that people will understand the distinctions,
respond appropriately, or avoid warning fatigue. The authority workflow is
complete as a technical boundary within the thesis scope; its human
effectiveness remains an empirical question.

\section{Limitations and validity}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

The principal security limitation concerns adaptation. Several attack families
informed detector and representation changes during development, including a
WASP failure in which an unsafe link target was absent from BrowserUse's
accessibility text. The component scorecards therefore demonstrate regression
coverage of known families, not generalisation to unseen attacks. The final
three-seed experiment has a stronger boundary because its middleware and
condition-specific policies were frozen before execution. Even there, however,
a clean post-sanitisation check establishes only that the configured detector no
longer fires; it does not prove that the remaining observation is free of
hostile intent. A held-out adaptive study against the frozen whole system is
needed to test that stronger claim. Browser-specific evaluation could follow
MUZZLE by selecting injection surfaces from agent behaviour and refining attacks
through execution feedback~\cite{zhan2025adaptive,syros2026muzzle}.

The observation and outcome measures impose further construct limits. WASP's
action audit recognises instantiated attacker goals but not every equivalent
effect or reasoning-only diversion. Moreover, screenshots were excluded from
the model's WASP observation path. The results consequently apply to the
evaluated textual channel and do not extend to pixel-only instructions or tasks
that require visual grounding. A visual path would need its own provenance,
isolation, and action-authority design because images expand both capability and
attack surface~\cite{bailey2024imagehijacks,koh2024visualwebarena}.

Experimental validity is bounded by the selected models, benchmarks, and
execution design. AgentDojo contributes three matched model configurations,
whereas the final WASP comparison uses one 27B model, three sampling seeds, and
a fixed 84-case matrix. Website state was reset before every cell, but condition
order was not randomised; changes in service load or model-serving state could
therefore coincide with a later condition. The historical 9B WASP rows remain
development evidence, and some AgentDojo artefacts predate the final provenance
schema. In addition, AgentDojo enforcement combines external guards with fixed
system-level security guidance, so its rates describe the governed
configuration as a whole rather than the guards in isolation. These constraints
preclude population-level deployment claims, causal model-scaling claims, and
fine-grained attribution of every observed effect.

Finally, the policy and prototype boundaries are finite. Pattern-based
protected-data classification can miss a semantically sensitive value, while a
permitted same-origin action may trigger server-side consequences unavailable
to local inspection. Loopback origin checks are appropriate for the local
prototype but do not isolate it from a compromised same-user process. Incidents,
secret takeover, live revalidation, extraction and history handling, and
special-URL controls have current test coverage, yet the frozen benchmark
trajectories did not measure their effects. They remain implementation assurance
rather than retrospective experimental evidence.

\subsection{Ethics and responsible research}
\label{subsec:ethics}

No human participated in the reported studies, and no participant data were
collected. Scripted benchmark modes supplied reproducible control behaviour, so
approval counts, pause duration, and Activity annotations are technical
telemetry. Likewise, interface walkthroughs establish server-owned request
semantics and rendered states, not comprehension, trust, workload, habituation,
or recovery quality.

The browser evidence came from controlled benchmark environments and local
synthetic interface fixtures. The submitted figures contain no live credential,
private account, private URL, or raw attack payload, and the dissertation does
not reproduce the benchmark's operational injection text. These choices reduce
unnecessary disclosure, although the work remains dual use because the analysis
can inform attacks as well as defences. Any future study of operator decisions
would therefore require ethics approval before recruitment or data collection,
together with protocols for consent, data minimisation, and secure handling of
records.

\section{Research priorities and broader implications}

The limitations suggest an order for future work rather than an undifferentiated
list of extensions. The first priority is a frozen, held-out adaptive evaluation
of the combined boundaries using unseen prompt families, a larger seed set, and
randomised or counterbalanced condition order. Adding a genuine action-only
comparator would also test whether early observation intervention preserves
useful trajectories more effectively than recognising harmful effects after the
model has planned them. This experiment would address the most important open
security and causal questions before expanding the prototype's capabilities.

The second priority is to broaden what can be governed. A visual guard would
need bounded, provenance-aware representations and stricter authority for
uncertain pixel-derived actions. In parallel, an operator-visible task contract
could state expected origins, data classes, permitted effects, human steps, and
stop conditions before exposure to untrusted content. Coupling that contract to
an isolated semantic monitor would test whether task-level reasoning can close
gaps left by deterministic policy without creating prohibitive false
interventions. Each mechanism would require its own evaluation rather than
borrowing confidence from the present textual results.

Human and deployment questions should follow the same evidential discipline. A
participant study could test whether operators understand interventions,
distinguish among approval, direct action, and incident review, and resume work
without excessive burden or warning fatigue. The implemented control plane is a
technical basis for that study, not evidence of those outcomes. Deployment
beyond the local prototype would separately require authenticated control,
stronger process separation, protected policy and trace keys, and support for
transactional browser environments. Better human interaction, broader attack
coverage, and stronger isolation are related goals, but evidence for one cannot
substitute for the others.

The broader implication is therefore architectural rather than universal.
Under the tested conditions, useful autonomy and independent authority were
compatible: Sentinel constrained what reached and left the planner without
requiring a perfect classifier or preventing the agent from completing useful
work. The results do not remove the need for stronger semantic representations,
adaptive testing, or human evaluation. They do show why authority need not be
entrusted to the same probabilistic component that plans the task.
