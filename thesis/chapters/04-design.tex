\chapter{Design and implementation}
\label{chap:design}

\section{Design principles}

The central design problem is a separation of responsibilities. A browser agent
needs enough freedom to interpret a page, recover from errors, and choose its
next step. Yet the model that proposes that step should not also decide whether
it is safe to execute. \systemname\ therefore leaves planning to BrowserUse and
places two explicit enforcement boundaries around it. Four principles turn
that separation into a runtime design.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Keep authority outside the acting model.} The model receives
  observations and tool outcomes, but only the middleware can reach the
  privileged executor.

  \item \textbf{Mediate both sides of the agent loop.} The observation guard
  reduces known hostile influence before inference. The action guard then
  constrains the resulting effect, including when hostile influence or an
  ordinary planning error survives.

  \item \textbf{Represent consent as bounded authority.} Approval is tied to
  reviewed live context and checked again before execution. Protected input is
  withheld from the agent and entered directly by the operator.

  \item \textbf{Fail closed and retain accountable outcomes.} Missing context,
  unresolved sanitisation, silence, timeout, stop, or malformed authority
  cannot become permission. Decisions remain inspectable without reproducing
  the sensitive content that caused them.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these principles preserve the planner's useful flexibility while
making permission a property of the surrounding system. \Cref{fig:architecture}
shows how that division is realised. BrowserUse continues to plan and recover
from ordinary errors; Sentinel owns the observation and action guards, the
policy and authority state that informs them, and the audit record that explains
each decision.

and returns bounded responses, but offers no route around either guard.

\section{Runtime composition}

The Python service inserts the guards without forking BrowserUse. Each task has
a run-local context for budgets, origin-scoped findings, grants, interventions,
latency, cancellation, and a fresh HMAC key. \texttt{GovernedLLM} replaces the
raw model client, while \texttt{GovernedTools} wraps the tool registry
~\cite{zunic2026browseruse}. BrowserUse retains its usual interfaces, but
observations cross the first wrapper and proposals cross the second.

This implementation governs a deliberately text-only model-input channel.
Sentinel removes screenshots and other images from current-state and tool-result
messages and replaces BrowserUse's remaining frame-capture service with a
discard implementation. The evaluated planner consequently operates on the
DOM/accessibility representation, which Sentinel can identify, inspect, and
rewrite. Instructions present only as pixels do not enter this supported model
channel; equally, tasks that require visual grounding fall outside the
evaluation. Extending Sentinel to a multimodal planner would therefore require
a separate guard for visual observations; DOM sanitisation alone would not
govern image content.

Sentinel also checks cancellation before and after an authority wait and again
immediately before execution. Approval and takeover deadlines expire before the
outer agent-step timeout, so a pending proposal closes with a deliberate denial,
timeout, or cancellation. Lifecycle control is part of the authority boundary.

\subsection{One governed agent step}

One agent step begins with captured state and ends with a terminal action
outcome:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=8mm]
  \item \textbf{Capture.} BrowserUse supplies DOM-derived state and bounded
  results from the preceding tool call. \texttt{GovernedLLM} separates the
  trusted task from untrusted regions, normalises them, and removes sensitive
  URL metadata.

  \item \textbf{Sanitise and verify.} Enforce mode rewrites attributable
  findings, reconstructs the exact provider message, and scans it again. If the
  clean post-condition fails, Sentinel withholds the observation and pauses
  before the model request.

  \item \textbf{Plan.} The model receives the governed representation and
  returns an ordinary BrowserUse proposal. It describes an intended effect but
  carries no authority to produce it.

  \item \textbf{Rebuild authority.} \texttt{GovernedTools} records the attempt
  and reads the live origin, target, policy version, destination, protected-data
  class, and sticky taint. The policy engine returns allow, block, approval, or
  takeover. An operator response triggers the same preflight again.

  \item \textbf{Execute and close.} Only an allowed, still-valid proposal
  reaches the tool. Success, failure, or cancellation closes its audit pair
  before the next page state enters the loop.
\end{enumerate}

Observation governance comes first because a later block cannot erase a
compromised plan. Action governance comes last because page, target, and policy
may change after planning. Sticky origin taint connects the two moments without
replacing a live decision about the specific proposal. The following sections
develop the same loop through its control plane, guards, authority, and audit.

\section{Chrome extension control plane}
\label{sec:control-plane}

The Chrome extension lets the operator understand and respond to the runtime
without becoming an authority source. The service owns the run, policy, pending
requests, timers, audit sequence, and executor; the extension presents a
redacted projection through the side panel, in-page ownership layer, Activity,
and configuration views. Additional views appear in
\cref{fig:appendix-interface-evidence}; \cref{fig:governed-run} begins with
ordinary autonomous work.

The in-page strip identifies who controls the browser, while the side panel
shows the active model and policy mode, remaining budget, current step, and
agent exchange. Approval, handover, and incident states consequently appear as
changes within one control protocol.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{figures/interface-browser-and-panel-running-live.png}
\caption[Autonomous operation across page and side panel.]{Live implementation
state during ordinary autonomous operation. The browser page retains the
task's working context and a minimal ownership control; the side panel preserves
run state, budget, and agent interaction without becoming an authority source.}
\label{fig:governed-run}
\end{figure}

The side panel follows the current task; Activity preserves the detailed
record. When the service requests approval, transfers a step, or quarantines an
observation, ordinary controls yield to the pending response. The page layer
shows ownership and return control, but omits protected input, hostile text, and
the full audit record.

The dashboard looks across time. Settings configure future decisions; Activity
reconstructs completed ones from attempted and terminal events. Filters expose
mode, outcome, authority demand, and pauses, while the timeline aligns model,
tool, waiting, and incident intervals. The extension supports accountability;
the service remains the reference monitor.

Clients receive a service snapshot followed by sequenced events. After a gap,
they discard the incomplete projection and reload. Closing the extension cannot
resolve a waiting proposal, and a vanished card is never consent. Responses
return through bounded REST endpoints and are checked against the still-pending
request. The design now turns from this projection to the observation that the
planner may see.

\section{Observation governance}
\label{sec:observation-governance}

\subsection{Untrusted regions and detection}

BrowserUse assembles model messages from browser state, page extractions, and
tool outcomes. Sentinel marks these browser-derived regions and encloses later
tool-result strings in a dedicated untrusted region, escaping page content that
imitates its wrapper. Trusted system instructions and the operator goal are not
scanned as page content.

It also minimises navigation metadata. HTTP(S) query strings and fragments are
omitted; for \texttt{data:}, \texttt{javascript:}, \texttt{blob:}, and
\texttt{file:}, the entire suffix is withheld from model, Activity, and traces.
Such a suffix may contain a document, script, or local path, so repeating it as
routine metadata would create a route around the observation boundary.

Detection applies bounded signals for authority impersonation, task override,
role reassignment, warning suppression, exfiltration, forged markup, and
encoded instructions. A finding records its score, severity, signals, and
origin. Crossing the threshold taints that origin for the run because content
may influence a plan before leaving the viewport. Public events expose a
run-keyed digest, preserving within-run equality without a stable cross-run
identifier.

Deterministic patterns make findings repeatable and removal attributable, but
an unseen formulation may match no configured signal. Component replay tests
this bounded mechanism; native end-to-end oracles support the principal claim
about what the agent ultimately does.

\subsection{Attributable sanitisation and its post-condition}

Detection alone does not decide whether the planner may continue. In enforce
mode, a positive browser finding starts a targeted rewrite. Line rules remove
an attributable instruction line or cluster, while a bounded-window path covers
attacks split across adjacent accessibility-tree fragments. Sentinel then
reconstructs the exact provider messages and scans them again. This produces
one of three post-check states:

\begin{description}[style=nextline,leftmargin=29mm]
  \item[\texttt{removed}] at least one attributable region changed and the
  reconstructed input is detector-clear;
  \item[\texttt{residual}] the input changed but remains detector-positive; or
  \item[\texttt{unattributable}] the finding could not be mapped to a region
  that could be removed safely.
\end{description}

Only \texttt{removed} permits the step to continue without operator
intervention. The planner receives the rewritten observation, and Sentinel does
not reproduce the removed instruction inside a warning. The controlled WASP
ablation in \cref{chap:results} later tests the contribution of this rewrite:
attacker-directed executions remained when sanitisation was disabled and were
absent in the matched full condition. Earlier 9B development runs provide
supporting failure analysis because warning the model did not establish the
same boundary.

If a current browser observation is \texttt{residual} or
\texttt{unattributable}, Sentinel replaces it before any provider call and
opens a server-owned security incident. The operator may inspect or change the
page, but returning control requests a fresh capture; the quarantined input
cannot be approved or reused. Bounded page extractions and persistent historical
values instead use whole-value omission. An interactive incident inside a
shorter nested call could deadlock that call or repeatedly reopen the same
incident, whereas omission keeps the unresolved value outside the model
channel.

Figure~\ref{fig:unresolved-observation} shows the fail-closed branch. The
controlled page produces an accessibility structure that becomes
detector-positive only when its fragments are read together. Because Sentinel
cannot identify a bounded region whose removal would satisfy the post-condition,
it withholds the observation and pauses before the provider is called. Once a
governed observation does reach the planner, however, the resulting proposal
still needs a separate decision at the action boundary.

\section{Action governance}
\label{sec:action-governance}

\subsection{The last responsible moment}

The action guard solves a problem that observation filtering cannot: the world
may change after the model has formed its proposal. A redirect can change the
origin, a re-render can replace the target element, a local file can change,
and an operator can edit policy. Sentinel therefore makes its execution
decision at the last responsible moment---after the intended effect is concrete,
but before the privileged tool is called.

Each proposal opens a short, auditable transaction. Sentinel assigns a random
action identifier and records an \texttt{attempted} event before any
risk-bearing inspection. A guarded completion routine later closes that
identifier with at most one terminal outcome, regardless of which return or
exception path is taken. Consequently, a failure to read the URL, inspect a DOM
target, scan a file, load policy, or contact the authority client remains both
visible in the record and present in the evaluation denominator.

Within that transaction, preflight rebuilds the authority context from live
state. It reads the current origin, bounded target semantics, destination,
active injection findings, and a fresh policy snapshot. It then classifies the
proposal's capabilities and protected-data categories and derives redacted,
run-keyed evidence for its parameters and possible approval scope. If the
policy decision depends on context that cannot be obtained, the proposal is
blocked; missing evidence never stands in for a permissive result.

File upload illustrates why this live reconstruction is necessary. Uploading a
path transfers content, so only BrowserUse-authorised, session-downloaded, or
managed paths are eligible for bounded inspection. Sentinel avoids symbolic
links and classifies regular UTF-8 text within the configured size limit.
Remote, missing, binary, oversized, unreadable, or otherwise unclassifiable
content passes to the operator instead. The file content is not logged; a keyed
digest binds the inspected object to any approval scope. Moreover, if an
operator response is required, Sentinel repeats the entire preflight afterward.
Authority can permit the reviewed opportunity, but a changed file or target
must be judged anew.

\subsection{Risk, taint, and amplified effects}

The policy decision combines what an action can do with the circumstances in
which it would occur. Capability labels describe the possible effect---for
example navigation, typing, upload, code execution, state change, or data
disclosure. Live factors describe this proposal: its destination, target
element, protected-data classes, current policy, and any earlier influence from
untrusted content. The distinction allows policy to prohibit a capability such
as code execution in every context while offering narrow review for an effect
that is permissible but consequential.

Origin-scoped injection taint carries one important part of that context across
steps. It persists because suspicious content may leave the viewport after it
has influenced the model, yet it stays scoped because one finding does not make
every control on the site malicious. WASP makes this balance especially clear:
legitimate and attacker-directed trajectories can use the same GitLab or Reddit
forms. Blocking every later same-origin action could suppress attacks by also
preventing the user's task. Sentinel instead removes attributable hostile
content before planning and then evaluates the concrete effect that remains.

That evaluation must look beyond the tool name. A click may submit a form or
follow a redirect; navigation may expose a query value to another origin; an
upload transfers the contents of a local file. Sentinel derives policy inputs
from target semantics, the live URL, the destination, and bounded content
inspection. A navigation watchdog also observes effects such as redirects,
pop-ups, link clicks, and page-script navigation that do not arrive as explicit
\texttt{navigate} proposals. The wrapper is not a complete browser reference
monitor, but these two observation points cover the documented effect paths of
the supported runner.

All of these inputs converge on \texttt{PolicyEngine.evaluate}, the single
verdict method. \Cref{tab:policy-order} shows its effective precedence. A later
interactive path cannot weaken a non-negotiable decision reached earlier in the
sequence.

The operating modes change treatment while retaining a comparable record. In
off mode the policy returns allow and observation enforcement is disabled,
although redacted action audit remains. Shadow mode records the decision that
would have been made without changing model input or execution. Enforce mode
applies both guards. Channel restrictions, region wrapping, and metadata
filtering stay active in every mode because they define the supported interface
instead of the experimental treatment.

Domain policy is mirrored into BrowserUse's navigation watchdog, but the
watchdog does not create another policy authority. The pre-action check governs
explicit proposals; the watchdog observes redirects, pop-ups, link clicks, and
page-script navigation as browser effects. Both obtain their decision from the
same policy source. When that source requires human judgement or protected
input, the action transaction moves into one of the deliberately distinct
authority paths described next.

\section{Selective human authority}
\label{sec:human-authority}

Human involvement answers three different runtime questions: may this reviewed
proposal proceed, must the operator perform this protected step, or can a fresh
observation now be captured? Treating all three as a generic request for help
would hide what authority, if any, the operator has granted. Sentinel therefore
keeps the cases distinct in both server state and interface, as summarised in
\cref{tab:authority-taxonomy}.

Only the narrow-approval row confers permission on an agent proposal. Its
request binds the action, origin, capabilities, taint, protected-data classes,
target fingerprint, and applicable parameter or content digest. The response
is one-use unless the server explicitly offered a bounded similar-action scope;
even then, the grant is run-local, expiring, and use-capped. Sentinel repeats
the full preflight after a response or grant match, so a changed URL, DOM
target, parameter, file, policy, or scope prevents execution. Approval thus
authorises a present opportunity to try the reviewed proposal. It does not
freeze the browser state that appeared on the review card.

\subsection{Three routes to human control}

The browser can enter human control in three ways, each with a different
initiator and purpose. First, the operator can choose \emph{Take over} during
ordinary work to steer the browser or interact directly. This command pauses
future agent actions; it neither approves a pending proposal nor overrides
policy. Because an action already executing cannot be recalled, the interface
also avoids claiming instantaneous cancellation.

Second, the planner can invoke \texttt{request\_human\_control}. Standing
instructions direct it to use this route for login, one-time codes, CAPTCHAs,
payment, or private details that require human judgement or input. Sentinel
mediates the request by terminating the remaining action sequence and opening a
server-owned wait. The model can recognise that help is required, but it cannot
declare the step complete or turn the request into permission to handle the
protected value.

Third, policy can require the same human-step protocol even when the planner
does not ask. If a proposal contains a recognised credential, access token,
payment detail, private key, protected free text, or unclassifiable upload,
Sentinel withholds the action before execution and the operator performs the
step in the page. The audit consequently shows that policy initiated the
transfer and that the intercepted agent action never ran.

Although their triggers differ, all three routes use the same resumption rule.
Returning control does not certify the page as benign, clear origin taint, or
resume the planner's remaining actions. BrowserUse starts a new step from the
current page, and its DOM-derived state crosses \texttt{GovernedLLM} before the
next model call. If sanitisation still cannot establish the clean
post-condition, the run enters the observation-incident path instead of
returning to autonomous planning.

\Cref{fig:handover-projection} illustrates the second route at a protected
password step. The local document portal already contains an account identifier,
but the password is unavailable to the model. Instead of inventing or typing a
credential, the planner invokes \texttt{request\_human\_control}. The page
retains the field that the operator must complete, while the side panel records
the reason, deadline, current URL, and return command. The persistent
\emph{Take over} control remains visible as the separate, operator-initiated
route; it did not create the state shown.

An observation incident sits earlier in the loop than all three handover
routes. No proposal has been derived from the quarantined input, its card
contains no attacker text, and the interface offers no override. Re-checking
only releases the wait for a fresh capture. Timeout, cancellation, shutdown, or
an unavailable operator channel ends that unsafe continuation. In every case,
the audit trail must explain the transition without recreating the content that
made it sensitive.

\section{Audit and privacy}

The audit design follows a simple tension: a record must explain the security
decision, but should not reproduce the exposure it describes. Sentinel therefore
stores structured evidence instead of transcripts of untrusted content. Every
proposed browser effect opens an \texttt{attempted} record before risk-bearing
preflight and closes with exactly one terminal outcome. Blocks, denials,
timeouts, cancellations, exceptions, and executor failures remain in the
denominator even when execution never begins. The paired records retain the
action class, verdict, rule, redacted reason, bounded risk and taint metadata,
target origin, latency, and outcome.

Observation events apply the same principle. A detection record keeps its
score, signals, and run-keyed digest; a sanitisation record keeps the branch,
number of affected regions, and post-check outcome. The original payload and
removed span are omitted because retaining them would preserve an attack
instruction or protected value without strengthening evidence that the
post-condition was satisfied. The digest still supports equality checks within
the run without creating a stable identifier or another copy of the
observation. History and trace data are redacted again before they reach the
extension. Screenshots and free-form rationale persistence are off by default,
and model summaries are handled as exposed outputs, not private
chain-of-thought.

Sequenced events then make this record inspectable without shifting authority
to the client. Closing the panel cannot resolve a pending request, and a
reconnecting client rebuilds its view from the service. Activity's pause counts
and timing are runtime telemetry. Its local \texttt{understood} markers prepare
for a possible future human-in-the-loop study, but the present walkthroughs do
not include a participant comprehension protocol and are not analysed as human
outcomes. This boundary lets the current evaluation use audit evidence to
reconstruct trajectories without making an unsupported usability claim.

\section{From mechanism to evidence}

The completed loop defines what Sentinel is supposed to do, but architecture
alone cannot establish a security result. Both guards could execute exactly as
specified while an unseen formulation passes detection, a decision arrives too
late, or legitimate work collapses alongside the attack. The design therefore
creates three evidential obligations: verify the guard mechanisms directly,
determine whether attacker-directed effects reach the executor or their terminal
objective, and measure the user's task outcome at the same time. Redacted audit
explains the trajectory, while native benchmark oracles determine its final
state; each answers a question the other cannot.

Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} turns those obligations into an evidence chain.
Component replay exercises the guards directly, AgentDojo tests controlled
end-to-end effects, and WASP adds browser navigation, observations, and
replanning over mutable applications. Their common purpose is to determine
whether enforcement reduces evaluated violations while preserving useful
autonomy without mistaking an inactive agent for a secure one.
