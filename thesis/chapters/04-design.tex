\chapter{Design and implementation}
\label{chap:design}

\section{Design principles and architecture}

The central design problem is to separate planning from permission. A browser
agent needs latitude to interpret pages, recover from errors, and choose its
next step. However, the model proposing that step should not also decide whether
its effect is permitted. \systemname\ therefore leaves planning to BrowserUse
while independently governing what reaches the planner and the privileged
executor. Four principles turn this separation into a runtime architecture.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Keep authority outside the acting model.} The model receives
  observations and tool outcomes, but only the middleware can pass its proposals
  to the privileged executor.

  \item \textbf{Mediate both sides of the agent loop.} The observation guard
  reduces known hostile influence before inference; the action guard constrains
  any resulting effect when hostile influence or planning error survives.

  \item \textbf{Represent consent as bounded authority.} Approval is tied to
  reviewed live context and rechecked before execution. Protected input is
  withheld from the agent and entered directly by the operator.

  \item \textbf{Fail closed and retain accountable outcomes.} Missing context,
  unresolved sanitisation, operator silence, timeout, cancellation, or
  malformed authority cannot become permission. Decisions remain inspectable
  without reproducing the sensitive content that caused them.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these principles preserve planning flexibility while making
permission a system property. BrowserUse plans and recovers from ordinary
errors; Sentinel owns the guards, authority, and audit that decide whether a
proposal may take effect (\cref{fig:architecture}).

Read from left to right, the trusted task reaches the planner, whose proposal
must cross the action guard before the executor can change the browser. In the
opposite direction, page and tool data cross the observation guard before
reaching the planner. Policy and bounded human authority inform both decisions;
the audit path records their outcomes.

The chapter follows this path through observation, action, authority, and audit,
then shows how the Chrome extension projects server-owned state into the browser
without becoming a second source of permission.

\section{Runtime architecture}

\subsection{Composition and supported channel}

Sentinel runs as a Python service around an ordinary BrowserUse agent, without
forking BrowserUse. A model wrapper governs the planner's observations, while a
tool wrapper mediates each proposal immediately before execution. Their
implementation classes, \texttt{GovernedLLM} and \texttt{GovernedTools}, retain
BrowserUse's normal interfaces~\cite{zunic2026browseruse}.

Each task has its own budgets, findings, grants, intervention and cancellation
state, counters, and HMAC key. Thus, authority cannot carry into a later task,
and digests cannot link identical audited values across runs.

The supported model-input channel is deliberately textual. Sentinel removes
images and remaining frame captures, marks browser-derived text as untrusted,
and reduces sensitive URL metadata. The evaluated planner receives a
DOM/accessibility representation that Sentinel can inspect and rewrite. This
excludes pixel-only instructions and visually grounded tasks; a multimodal
planner would require a separate visual-observation guard.

Sentinel exposes three runtime modes, distinguished by whether security
decisions are evaluated and enforced. Off skips prompt-injection detection and
makes the action policy return allow, although redacted action attempts and
terminal outcomes are still audited. Shadow runs the detector and action-policy
rules against live context, but neither rewrites the checked model input nor
applies a policy-derived verdict. Instead, it records the detections and the
block, approval, or handoff that would have occurred. Enforce applies those
outcomes: it may sanitise or withhold a detected observation; for an action
proposal, it may allow, block, request approval, or require a human step. In the
evaluation, \emph{full} denotes enforce mode with both guards enabled; it is an
experimental condition, not a fourth runtime mode. Across every mode, the
supported model channel remains text-only, tool results remain marked as
untrusted, and navigation metadata remains redacted.

Sentinel checks cancellation around every authority wait and immediately before
execution. Its deadlines expire before BrowserUse's outer step timeout, so
denial, silence, cancellation, or shutdown closes the pending action.

\subsection{One governed agent step}

A BrowserUse step may contain a short, ordered action sequence. Sentinel treats
each action as a separate proposal with its own authority decision and terminal
audit outcome:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=8mm]
  \item \textbf{Capture.} The observation wrapper separates the trusted task
  from DOM-derived state and bounded tool results, normalises the representation,
  and removes sensitive URL metadata.

  \item \textbf{Sanitise and verify.} Enforce mode rewrites attributable
  findings, reconstructs the provider message, and scans it again. Sentinel
  withholds any message that is not detector-clear.

  \item \textbf{Plan.} The model receives the governed representation and
  returns proposals that describe intended effects but carry no permission.

  \item \textbf{Rebuild authority.} The action wrapper records each attempt and
  reconstructs its live origin, destination, target, policy, protected-data
  classes, and taint. Policy may allow, block, request approval, or require a
  human step. Approval triggers complete preflight again; a human step withholds
  the proposal for direct operator action.

  \item \textbf{Execute and close.} Only an allowed, still-valid proposal
  reaches the tool. Its audit pair closes before the next proposal; an error,
  sequence-terminating action, or URL or focus change abandons the remainder.
\end{enumerate}

Observation governance comes first, since a later block cannot undo a plan
already shaped by hostile content. Nevertheless, every proposal is governed
again because its page, target, policy, or data may change after planning.

\section{Observation governance}
\label{sec:observation-governance}

\subsection{Untrusted regions and representation boundaries}

Sentinel distinguishes trusted task context from browser state, extractions, and
tool results, scanning only these untrusted regions for prompt injection. Before
textual tool results enter agent history, it escapes reserved protocol tags and
places the results inside an explicit untrusted wrapper.

A page may imitate BrowserUse or Sentinel prompt structure. \emph{Boundary
neutralisation} therefore escapes reserved tags only inside recognised
untrusted regions. Genuine outer wrappers and ordinary DOM tags remain
unchanged, so forged syntax cannot create a trusted region or close the real
one.

Model-facing browser state also withholds page titles and replaces complete
HTTP(S) query strings and fragments. For \texttt{data:}, \texttt{javascript:},
\texttt{blob:}, \texttt{file:}, and other non-web URLs, only the scheme crosses
model, event, and trace boundaries; the opaque suffix may contain a document,
script, path, or identifier. Other telemetry retains useful HTTP(S) context
while redacting recognised sensitive values.

\subsection{Detection and attributable sanitisation}

The detector combines signals for instruction override, authority
impersonation, role reassignment, evasion, exfiltration, forged markup, and
encoded instructions. One strong signal can cross the threshold; weaker cues
must combine.
A detected origin remains tainted for the run because an earlier observation
may already have influenced the plan. Public events report the score, severity,
signals, and origin, replacing the stable content hash with a run-keyed digest
that links repeats only within that run.

For a positive finding, Sentinel first removes only attributable material. A
line rule handles a self-contained instruction, a bounded window covers an
instruction split across neighbouring accessibility-tree fragments, and
structural sanitisation provides a coarser third path.

Sentinel then rebuilds and rescans the exact provider message, producing three
states:

\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}l@{\hspace{1.5em}}Y@{}}
  \texttt{removed} & at least one attributable region changed and the
  reconstructed input is detector-clear; \\
  \texttt{residual} & the input changed but remains detector-positive; or \\
  \texttt{unattributable} & the finding could not be mapped to a region that
  could be removed safely.
\end{tabularx}

The pattern rules make findings repeatable and removal attributable. However,
an unfamiliar formulation may match no signal, and a clean post-check shows
only that the configured detector no longer fires, not that every possible
attack is absent.

\subsection{Fail-closed unresolved content}

Under the evaluated full configuration, \texttt{removed} is the only positive
detection outcome that continues to the planner. The rewritten observation
carries no warning, but all subsequent actions remain policy-gated.

A \texttt{residual} or \texttt{unattributable} current observation is replaced
by a fixed marker and held before the model call. Sentinel opens an observation
incident, so the provider is not called. If the operator requests a re-check,
Sentinel discards the held prompt and captures the page afresh; quarantined input
is never treated as approved.

For bounded extractions and persistent tool results, a pause could expire a
nested call or reopen the same historical incident. Sentinel therefore omits
the entire affected value.

These controls reduce known hostile influence but cannot guarantee a safe or
correct plan. Every resulting proposal still needs an independent action
decision.

\section{Action governance}
\label{sec:action-governance}

\subsection{Pre-execution reconstruction}

Context can change between planning and execution: a redirect may replace the
origin, a re-render may change the target, and a local file or policy may be
updated. Sentinel therefore decides at the last responsible moment, after the
effect is concrete but before the privileged tool is called.

Each proposal opens an auditable transaction. An action identifier and
\texttt{attempted} record precede fallible preflight; a guarded completion
routine adds exactly one terminal outcome. Executions, blocks, denials,
timeouts, cancellations, and errors therefore share one denominator.

Preflight reconstructs origin, destination, active findings, policy, and any
available bounded target semantics. It classifies capabilities and
protected-data categories, then derives redacted parameter and approval-scope
evidence. Missing URL context required by domain policy blocks the proposal;
other preflight exceptions also close it without execution.

File upload illustrates why live reconstruction matters. Sentinel inspects only
authorised, session-downloaded, or managed paths containing bounded regular
UTF-8 text. A remote, missing, binary, oversized, unreadable, or unclassifiable
file triggers a human step; the intercepted upload does not execute. For a
classifiable file, a keyed digest binds the unlogged content to approval scope.
Complete preflight repeats after approval, revealing changes to the file,
target, origin, or policy.

\subsection{Risk, taint, and policy precedence}

The action guard classifies effects such as navigation, page change,
disclosure, file access, and code execution, then combines them with live
origin, target, protected-data classes, policy, and taint. For example, policy
can prohibit \texttt{evaluate} while holding a consequential click for narrow
review.

Origin-scoped taint carries a finding across steps without following the agent
to unrelated origins or automatically blocking every same-site control. Thus,
Sentinel can assess each effect even when legitimate and attacker-directed
trajectories use the same form.

An action name does not describe its complete effect: a click may submit or
navigate, a URL may disclose a query across origins, and an upload transfers a
local file. Risk therefore depends on live target, URL, destination, and bounded
content. Effects outside the BrowserUse integration remain outside Sentinel's
claim.

These inputs converge on \texttt{PolicyEngine.evaluate}. As
\cref{tab:policy-order} shows, hard prohibitions precede human-step and approval
paths, so an interactive response cannot weaken them.

Only narrow approval can confer permission on a proposal. A human step reserves
direct action for the operator, an observation incident grants no override, and
a hard refusal remains final.

\subsection{Narrow approval and live revalidation}

Approval binds the reviewed action, origin, capabilities, taint, protected-data
classes, target, and parameter or content evidence. It applies once unless the
server offers a run-local, expiring, use-capped similar-action scope. After a
response or grant match, Sentinel repeats full preflight against current policy
and browser state. A new block, human-step requirement, or context mismatch
withholds execution: approval authorises a present opportunity, not frozen
browser state.

A per-run budget caps approval prompts, then invokes the configured human-step
or block fallback. Pending authority remains server-owned across extension
disconnects: a new connection receives current state and pending requests
before sequenced events. A vanished card never counts as consent; only a bounded
response to a still-pending request changes its state.

\subsection{Routes to direct human control}

Direct human control begins through three distinct routes. First, operator
\emph{Take over} pauses future agent actions without approving a proposal or
changing policy; an executing action cannot be recalled.

Second, the planner may invoke \texttt{request\_human\_control} for a login,
one-time code, CAPTCHA, payment, or private detail. Sentinel terminates the
remaining sequence and opens a server-owned wait that the model can neither
resolve nor use to acquire protected-value authority.

Third, policy forces the same handoff for recognised protected data,
unclassifiable uploads, first contact with a sensitive domain, or
approval-budget fallback. Sentinel withholds the proposal and records that it
did not execute.

Resumption depends on the route. Operator takeover continues at BrowserUse's
next check. A planner request has terminated its sequence, so the next step
captures fresh page state. A policy handoff reports that the protected proposal
did not execute; later proposals cross the guards again. In every case, the next
observation still crosses \texttt{GovernedLLM} and may trigger an incident.

After a completed sensitive-domain handoff, Sentinel suppresses repeats for that
pattern during the run without clearing taint or creating a general exemption.
Silence, cancellation, or shutdown keeps the unsafe continuation closed.

\section{Audit and privacy}

Sentinel's action audit stores structured evidence, not raw transcripts. Each
proposal receives paired \texttt{attempted} and terminal records, keeping
blocks, denials, timeouts, cancellations, exceptions, and executor failures in
one denominator. The pair retains action class, verdict, bounded risk and taint,
origin, latency, and outcome; enforcement adds the rule and redacted reason.

Observation records follow the same principle. Detection records score,
signals, and run-keyed digest; sanitisation records branch, affected-region
count, and post-check. Raw payloads and removed spans are omitted, while the
digest links repeats within one run without preserving content or creating a
stable identifier.

Before storage or extension delivery, Sentinel redacts task, action, URL, and
summary fields again. Extension events omit screenshots and page titles;
persistent screenshot and free-form rationale storage is opt-in and disabled by
default. The record still distinguishes proposal, execution, intervention, and
terminal outcome for trajectory analysis.

These events inform the operator without shifting authority into the client.
The Chrome extension is the control plane for this projection.

\section{Chrome extension: operator-facing control plane}
\label{sec:control-plane}

\subsection{Client--server authority boundary}

Sentinel can pause a run for approval, direct human action, or observation
review. The Chrome extension lets the operator inspect these server-owned states
and submit bounded responses, but never becomes another source of authority.
The service owns the run, policy, requests, timers, audit order, task lifecycle,
and privileged executor; the extension displays a redacted projection.

A Manifest V3 service worker and content script provide in-page controls; the
side panel and dashboard use HTTP and WebSocket. They relay responses, but only
the service decides whether the run changes.

\subsection{Autonomous operation and browser ownership}

The side panel is the task-facing surface. Its composer starts a task, steers the
next step during a run, or begins a contextual follow-up after completion. The
panel reports the active model, remaining call budget, token use, current step,
and redacted agent exchange. \Cref{fig:governed-run} shows ordinary autonomous
operation before an intervention.

\subsection{Intervention features}

Intervention cards expose Sentinel's earlier authority distinctions. An
approval card offers only server-defined responses to a bounded proposal. A
human-step card explains why the agent yielded and lets the operator mark the
direct step complete. An observation-incident card contains no attacker text or
approval path; its re-check control requests a fresh capture from the service.

page's accessibility structure becomes detector-positive only when its
fragments are read together. No bounded removal satisfies the post-condition,
so Sentinel withholds the observation before calling the provider. The unchanged
0/60 model-call counter shows this pre-inference stop without disclosing its
trigger.

\subsection{Policy, Activity, and future HITL scope}

The dashboard separates next-task configuration from retrospective review. Its
Models tab configures the primary and fallback model for the next task.
Policies displays the active mode and configures domain and action rules,
protected data flows, approval behaviour, audit options, and budgets. Saved
policy changes apply at the next action boundary, even during a run, but cannot
decide an already pending request or bypass server validation.

Activity reconstructs completed runs from the service's audit record. Its
summary exposes policy mode, outcome, decision and intervention counts, and
wait and policy-check time; expansion reveals the service-sequenced log. A
non-allow decision can also carry an \texttt{understood} marker that records
whether its displayed reason was clear. In the deterministic walkthrough both
responses remain unset. The control is an unevaluated instrument, not evidence
of comprehension or usability.

\Cref{fig:authority-audit-evidence} connects the live and retrospective views
from one walkthrough run. Panel~(a) shows the redacted approval card while the
proposal is held; panel~(b) shows the resulting outcomes, timing, and decisions
in Activity after the run.

These surfaces provide technical groundwork for a future HITL study, but do not
by themselves make the prototype study-ready. Here, automated tests and
deterministic walkthroughs establish only fail-closed transitions, redacted
projection, reconnect handling, and audit reconstruction; they provide no
participant evidence about comprehension, usability, trust, workload, fatigue,
intervention burden, or decision quality.

\section{Design synthesis}

Together, these mechanisms give each requirement an enforcement point.
Observation governance supports R2; per-proposal mediation and policy implement
R1 and R3 at the executor; selective, expiring authority provides R4; and paired
redacted records and run-keyed evidence provide R5. They form one governed path:
a missed or incompletely removed observation does not eliminate action policy,
and an operator response cannot weaken a hard prohibition.

Sentinel is the enforcement architecture; the Chrome extension is its
operator-facing projection, exposing server-owned states and relaying bounded
responses without independent authority. This hierarchy also frames the
evidence that follows. Component checks examine mechanism contracts; AgentDojo
examines controlled end-to-end effects; WASP examines browser-native transfer;
and walkthroughs establish technical authority and control-plane behaviour.
\Cref{chap:methodology} defines these evaluations and their limits.
